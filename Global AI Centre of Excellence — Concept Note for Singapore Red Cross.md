# AI Wellness Initiative

## Concept Paper for Singapore Red Cross Senior Management
*For consideration of a 6–8 week discovery and design phase*

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is moving from a back-office technology into everyday community life. AI chatbots, AI tutors, deepfakes, and agentic systems are increasingly part of how children learn, how families communicate, how young adults study and socialise, and how workers manage their time and attention.

For Singapore Red Cross, this creates both a challenge and an opportunity. The challenge is that AI is becoming a new source of stress, anxiety, and vulnerability in the communities the organisation serves — particularly among youth, families, and volunteers. The opportunity is that Singapore Red Cross is well placed to address it: trusted by the community, embedded in schools and youth networks, experienced in volunteer-led education, and anchored by a clear Mental Health mission.

This paper proposes that Singapore Red Cross explore an **AI Wellness Initiative** as a new programme stream under the Mental Health pillar. The initiative would help communities build AI literacy, digital resilience, and healthy habits — not as a technology programme, but as a mental wellness and community education initiative.

**What this paper is not:** This is not a request for approval to launch a full-scale programme. It is a request to approve a **6–8 week discovery and design phase** during which Singapore Red Cross would validate need, map partners, assess governance requirements, estimate resourcing, and develop a scoped pilot proposal for senior management review.

**What Singapore Red Cross would not become:** The organisation would not be positioned as a clinical authority on AI, a technical regulator, or a provider of diagnosis, counselling, or therapy related to AI use. The initiative would provide education, literacy-building, resilience support, signposting, and preventive community education — delivered within clear clinical and safeguarding boundaries.

## 2. Strategic Rationale

### 2a. AI is becoming a community well-being issue

Generative and agentic AI are no longer specialist technologies. They are embedded in the tools that children use for homework, young adults use for study and social connection, parents encounter in children's apps and devices, and workers use for productivity and communication. This ubiquity creates new pressures on mental health and community resilience:

- **Youth and children** are interacting with AI chatbots, AI-generated content, and AI-mediated social environments before they have the critical thinking skills to evaluate what they encounter. The risks include emotional attachment to AI companions, exposure to deepfakes and manipulated media, distorted self-image from AI-curated content, and AI-enabled grooming or manipulation.
- **Parents and caregivers** are frequently less AI-literate than their children. In homes where AI tutors, AI companions, and AI-driven apps are normalised, parents lack the knowledge and confidence to set healthy boundaries or guide meaningful conversations about what their children are experiencing.
- **Young adults** lean on AI for study, work, and emotional support — creating dependence while bypassing critical judgment. The risk is not just poor academic or professional outcomes, but the gradual atrophy of independent thinking and emotional self-reliance.
- **Volunteers** themselves need AI literacy to support beneficiaries who are navigating AI-shaped stressors. Without this capability, volunteers may miss signals of AI-related distress or lack the tools to guide conversations effectively.

These are not distant future concerns. They are present-day community issues that fall within the scope of a mental wellness and community resilience organisation.

### 2b. Why existing approaches are insufficient

Singapore's established cyber wellness programmes cover screen time, cyberbullying, safe sharing, and responsible digital citizenship. These foundations are important and must continue. But they were designed for an internet built on human-generated content. They were not designed for AI systems that create content, mediate relationships, and act on people's behalf.

Generative and agentic AI introduce new categories of risk that traditional programmes do not address: AI-generated misinformation that is plausible but false; emotional dependence on AI companions that mimic empathy; automation bias that erodes independent judgment; AI-curated social environments that distort identity formation; and deepfake media that undermines shared reality.

Addressing these risks requires more than updated lesson plans. It requires a programme that understands both the technology and its psychological impact, that builds resilience as well as knowledge, and that operates through trusted community channels.

### 2c. The case for Singapore Red Cross

Singapore Red Cross is not a technology organisation. That is precisely why it is well suited to lead this initiative. When the subject is mental health, community education, and youth resilience, the messenger matters as much as the message.

The organisation brings several distinctive assets:

- **Community trust.** Singapore Red Cross is a known, values-aligned institution. When the topic is mental health, people engage differently with an organisation they already trust.
- **Established reach.** The organisation works across schools, youth groups, families, volunteers, and community networks. AI wellness education can be delivered through these existing channels rather than built from scratch.
- **Mental Health mission alignment.** The initiative addresses a root cause of emerging mental health stress — not as a tech add-on, but as a core community wellness topic that fits naturally within the existing Mental Health pillar.
- **Volunteer-led delivery model.** The programme can leverage Singapore Red Cross's volunteer network, giving volunteers meaningful new skills and extending the organisation's educational reach proportionally without proportional cost increases.
- **Preventive orientation.** The initiative is inherently preventive: building literacy and resilience before problems escalate, consistent with the Red Cross approach to community health.

The proposal does not suggest that Singapore Red Cross should immediately lead the national agenda on AI and mental wellness. It suggests that the organisation is well positioned to explore whether this is a meaningful new direction for its Mental Health pillar, and to develop a rigorous, evidence-based pilot before committing to scale.

| Dimension | Relevance to Singapore Red Cross |
|---|---|
| **Mission** | AI wellness is a community well-being issue that aligns with the Mental Health pillar and the organisation's preventive health orientation. |
| **Audience** | The programme serves the same populations that Singapore Red Cross already engages — youth, families, volunteers, and community members. |
| **Channels** | Existing relationships with schools, community partners, and volunteer networks reduce the cost and complexity of programme delivery. |
| **Trust** | As an established humanitarian organisation, Singapore Red Cross carries credibility in mental health and community education that technology companies and academic institutions do not. |

The key word is **explore**. Singapore Red Cross is invited to assess whether this initiative fits the organisation's strategic priorities, resourcing capacity, and risk appetite — not to commit to a multi-year programme before the case has been built.

## 4. Proposed Scope for Discovery Phase

If management approves, the 6–8 week discovery phase would have the following scope:

**In scope:**
- Review of the AI wellness landscape in Singapore: existing programmes, gaps, and potential collaborators
- Stakeholder mapping: schools, community partners, corporate sponsors, government agencies, and expert advisors who could advise, co-design, or support delivery
- Needs validation: initial engagement with a small number of schools, parents, youth, and volunteers to test whether the proposed programme topics resonate
- Governance review: identification of safeguarding, data protection, AI tool governance, and clinical boundary requirements
- Partner and resourcing assessment: high-level mapping of what could be delivered internally versus what requires external expertise or partnership
- Pilot curriculum outline: a draft framework for one or two priority programme tracks, including session structure, content themes, and delivery formats
- Risk register: initial identification of key risks and proposed mitigations
- Resource and budget estimate: indicative costing for a pilot phase
- Success criteria: definition of what a successful pilot would look like and how it would be measured

**Out of scope:**
- Programme delivery or pilot execution
- Commitment of funds beyond the discovery phase
- Appointment of programme staff
- Signing of partnership agreements
- Development of a full Centre of Excellence or national platform

The discovery phase would be led by a small internal working group with input from external advisors as needed. A final report would be presented to senior management with a clear recommendation: proceed to pilot, modify scope and revisit, or discontinue.

## 5. Priority Audiences for Initial Pilot

If the discovery phase validates the concept and management approves a pilot, the recommendation would be to start with one or two priority audiences rather than all four at once. This reduces risk, contains cost, and allows learning to inform later expansion.

**Recommended starting audiences:**

### Priority 1: Youth and Schools

Secondary and post-secondary students, delivered through school workshops, assemblies, and peer-facilitated sessions. This is the highest-priority audience because:

- Young people are the group most directly exposed to AI risks (AI chatbots, deepfakes, AI-mediated social environments) while having the least developed critical thinking skills
- Schools provide a structured, trusted delivery environment with established pastoral and safeguarding frameworks
- Peer-ambassador models leverage existing youth leadership structures within Singapore Red Cross
- Early success with youth creates a platform for parent and caregiver engagement, since children often raise these topics at home

**Programme content for youth would cover:** healthy AI habits, understanding AI chatbots and companions, deepfakes and digital trust, emotional regulation in AI-mediated environments, online boundaries, and agentic AI — what it is and how to stay in control.

### Priority 2: Parents and Caregivers

Delivered through school-based parent sessions, webinars, and family learning resources. This audience is a natural complement to the youth track because:

- Parents are often less AI-literate than their children, creating a guidance gap at home
- Engaging parents alongside youth multiplies the impact of school-based sessions
- Parent sessions can be layered onto existing school engagement relationships, reducing marginal delivery cost
- Family learning resources extend the programme's reach beyond attendees

**Programme content for parents would cover:** AI tools children are likely to encounter, setting family boundaries for AI and device use, recognising AI-influenced distress in children, conversation starters for difficult topics, and signposting to appropriate support services.

### Later-phase extensions (subject to pilot outcomes)

- **Young adults** (tertiary learners and young workers): campus seminars and practical labs, contingent on successful delivery with secondary-level audiences first
- **Working adults and frontline professionals:** workplace sessions, digital wellness clinics, and sector-specific programmes — positioned as a Phase 2 extension after the pilot has been evaluated and refined

## 6. Operating Model and Partner Roles

A clear operating model is essential to ensure Singapore Red Cross leads what it should lead, partners contribute what they are best placed to provide, and the organisation does not overreach into areas where it lacks expertise or mandate.

### What Singapore Red Cross would lead

| Area | Description |
|---|---|
| **Programme design and delivery** | Curriculum development, session facilitation, and participant engagement — drawing on the organisation's established education and community outreach capabilities |
| **Volunteer management** | Recruitment, training, supervision, and safeguarding of volunteer facilitators |
| **Community trust and relationships** | Leveraging existing relationships with schools, families, and community networks to enable programme delivery |
| **Brand and communications** | Positioning the initiative as a Mental Health pillar programme, with clear messaging about scope, boundaries, and intended outcomes |
| **Pilot governance and oversight** | Ensuring the programme operates within Singapore Red Cross's governance frameworks and safeguarding standards |
| **Impact measurement** | Tracking participant reach, satisfaction, knowledge gains, and behaviour change indicators |

### What expert partners should provide

| Area | Description |
|---|---|
| **AI and technology expertise** | Technical accuracy in curriculum content, advice on AI tool developments, and support with content review — provided by technology academics, AI governance practitioners, or industry advisors |
| **Mental health and clinical oversight** | Expert review of programme content for psychological safety, referral pathways, and escalation procedures — provided by qualified mental health professionals |
| **Youth and education expertise** | Input on age-appropriate content, school engagement, and youth facilitation — provided by education specialists and youth sector partners |
| **Research and evaluation** | Design of measurement frameworks, analysis of pilot outcomes, and identification of improvement areas — potentially provided by academic or evaluation partners |

### What schools, community partners, and volunteers would support

| Area | Description |
|---|---|
| **Programme venues and access** | Schools providing space, assembly time, or co-facilitation; community partners hosting sessions or referring participants |
| **Participant recruitment** | Schools, parents, and community partners helping to reach the right audiences |
| **Peer facilitation** | Trained youth volunteers facilitating peer-ambassador sessions |
| **Family engagement** | Parents and caregivers participating in sessions and providing feedback |
| **Beneficiary feedback** | Schools and community partners sharing observations on programme impact and areas for improvement |

### What should be outsourced or co-developed

| Area | Description |
|---|---|
| **Digital platform and online resources** | If an online hub or self-assessment tools are developed, technical implementation should be outsourced to a qualified provider rather than built internally |
| **Specialist AI tools or platforms** | Any AI tools demonstrated or referenced in the programme should be sourced, reviewed, and approved by external experts — not selected or endorsed unilaterally by Singapore Red Cross |
| **Research and evaluation** | External evaluation partners should be engaged to provide objective assessment of pilot outcomes |
| **Legal and regulatory advice** | PDPA compliance, data protection impact assessments, and liability considerations should involve qualified external legal counsel |

### What Singapore Red Cross should not do

Singapore Red Cross should not be positioned as:

- A **clinical authority** on AI or mental health — the organisation provides education and signposting, not diagnosis or therapy
- A **technical authority** on AI — technology expertise should come from expert advisors and partners
- A **regulatory or compliance authority** on AI — governance advice should come from qualified external sources
- An **unsupervised emotional support provider** — participants experiencing distress should be referred to qualified mental health services

This clarity protects the organisation's reputation, reduces liability, and ensures the initiative operates within its genuine areas of strength.

## 7. Safeguards, Governance and Boundaries

### 7a. Programme boundaries

The AI Wellness Initiative would provide:

- **Education and literacy** — helping participants understand AI systems, their capabilities, and their limitations
- **Resilience-building** — strengthening critical thinking, emotional regulation, and healthy digital habits
- **Signposting** — directing participants to appropriate support services for AI-related distress
- **Preventive support** — early intervention conversations and referrals before issues escalate

The initiative would **not** provide:

- Diagnosis of mental health conditions related to AI use
- Counselling, therapy, or clinical intervention
- Crisis intervention or emergency mental health response
- Unsupervised emotional support through AI tools or otherwise
- Technical advice or support for specific AI platforms
- Legal advice on AI governance or data protection

Any participant identified as needing clinical support, crisis intervention, or specialised services would be referred to appropriate qualified providers through established referral pathways.

### 7b. Safeguarding commitments

**Child and youth safeguarding:**
- All programme content for participants under 18 would be reviewed and approved by qualified safeguarding advisors before delivery
- Volunteer facilitators working with children and youth would be trained in child protection, mandatory reporting obligations, and Red Cross safeguarding standards
- Sessions with children and youth would be conducted in school or supervised community settings, never in isolated or unsupervised environments
- Parental consent would be obtained for all youth participants, with clear information about programme content and boundaries

**Vulnerable persons:**
- Programme materials would be designed to be accessible and inclusive, with consideration for participants with different abilities, backgrounds, and levels of digital access
- Facilitators would be trained to recognise signs of AI-related distress and to refer participants to appropriate support services
- The programme would not collect sensitive personal data from participants beyond what is necessary for programme evaluation

**Volunteer safeguarding:**
- All volunteer facilitators would undergo background screening in accordance with Singapore Red Cross policy
- Volunteers would receive training on programme content, safeguarding obligations, referral pathways, and appropriate boundaries with participants
- Volunteers would be supervised by experienced programme leads and would have clear escalation routes for safeguarding concerns
- Volunteers would not be placed in situations where they are expected to provide emotional support beyond their training and role definition

### 7c. Governance framework

| Governance Area | Proposed Approach |
|---|---|
| **Child safeguarding** | Programme content reviewed by safeguarding advisors; volunteer training on child protection; mandatory reporting procedures; school-based delivery settings |
| **Data protection (PDPA)** | Minimal data collection; informed consent for all data gathered; clear retention and deletion policies; data protection impact assessment for any digital tools used |
| **AI tool selection and approval** | Any AI tools demonstrated or referenced must be reviewed and approved by external AI governance advisors before use; tools must meet criteria for safety, data minimisation, age-appropriateness, and transparency |
| **Third-party platforms** | External platforms used for programme delivery or resources must be assessed for data protection, safeguarding, and content appropriateness; Singapore Red Cross would not endorse specific commercial products |
| **Crisis escalation and referral pathways** | Clear, documented referral pathways to qualified mental health services, school counsellors, and community support agencies; facilitators trained on escalation triggers and procedures |
| **Volunteer training and supervision** | Structured onboarding programme covering content knowledge, safeguarding, referral procedures, and boundary management; ongoing supervision by programme leads |
| **Reputational risk** | Clear communication about the initiative's scope and boundaries — Singapore Red Cross is an education and signposting partner, not a clinical or technical authority; all public communications would be reviewed before release |
| **Partner accountability** | Written agreements with all partners clarifying roles, responsibilities, safeguarding obligations, and governance standards; regular review of partner performance |
| **Content review** | All programme content reviewed by qualified mental health professionals and AI governance experts before delivery; curriculum updated as AI landscape evolves |

### 7d. Clinical and safeguarding boundaries in practice

To ensure clarity for management, participants, and partners:

- **Workshops and sessions** are educational and preventive. They are not therapy, counselling, or clinical intervention.
- **Discussion of AI-related distress** in sessions is acknowledged and met with appropriate signposting, not clinical response.
- **AI tools demonstrated** (if any, in later phases) are supervised, closed-scope, non-clinical, and do not collect sensitive personal data.
- **One-on-one conversations** between facilitators and participants about personal AI use or mental health concerns would be handled through established referral pathways, not kept within the programme.
- **Crisis situations** would be escalated immediately to qualified professionals via documented procedures — not managed within the programme.

## 9. Potential Pilot Success Measures

If the discovery phase leads to a pilot, the following measures would define success:

| Measure | Description |
|---|---|
| **Reach** | Number of participants engaged in pilot sessions, by audience type and delivery format |
| **Knowledge and confidence** | Pre- and post-session assessments measuring changes in participants' understanding of AI-related risks and their confidence in managing AI-related situations |
| **Satisfaction** | Participant and facilitator satisfaction scores collected after sessions |
| **Behavioural indicators** | Self-reported and observed changes in digital habits, conversation quality at home (for parent sessions), and help-seeking behaviour |
| **Volunteer development** | Number of volunteers trained, quality of facilitation, and volunteer retention and satisfaction |
| **Partner engagement** | Number and quality of partner organisations engaged, and their feedback on programme value and delivery |
| **Referral and escalation** | Number of participants referred to support services (indicating that the programme is reaching those who need it) and appropriate handling of safeguarding concerns |
| **Learning and iteration** | Evidence that pilot outcomes are being reviewed and used to refine programme content and delivery |
| **Feasibility of scale** | Assessment of whether the model can be refined and extended to additional audiences or settings |

These measures are deliberately practical and focused on learning. The pilot is an evidence-building exercise, not a public performance. Its success is measured by whether it generates reliable, actionable insight — not by scale of reach or media coverage.

## 11. Longer-Term Pathway, Subject to Validation

If the discovery phase and subsequent pilot validate the concept and management approves expansion, the longer-term pathway might include:

- **Phase 2 — Programme refinement and scale.** Based on pilot learning, refine the curriculum, expand to additional schools and parent groups, and build a volunteer facilitator network.
- **Phase 3 — Extended audiences.** Introduce young adult and working adult tracks, contingent on successful delivery with youth and parent audiences and availability of appropriate expertise and partnerships.
- **Phase 4 — Centre of Excellence consideration.** The "Centre of Excellence" positioning — including structured research, national partnerships, and regional knowledge-sharing — may be considered only after demand has been validated, partner ecosystems have been established, governance frameworks are mature, and resourcing is secure. This is a potential long-term ambition, not a starting point.
- **Regional knowledge-sharing.** If the programme proves effective, frameworks, curricula, and insights could be shared with other Red Cross / Red Crescent societies and international humanitarian partners — positioning Singapore Red Cross as a contributor to regional thinking on AI and community wellness, without premature claims of leadership.

None of these phases are pre-committed. Each would require its own management approval, evidence of impact from the preceding phase, and confirmation that governance, resourcing, and risk parameters remain acceptable.
